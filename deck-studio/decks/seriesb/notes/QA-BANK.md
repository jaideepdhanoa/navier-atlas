### 2026-08-06 — R25 (LC-180 storyline transfer)

**Q22. Can you really put a loaded freighter on foils?**
We don't have to. The cargo line is designed in two modes: light, urgent freight flies full-foil; heavy loads ride foil-assist, where the foils carry part of the vessel's weight and cut the power bill without lifting the hull clear of the water. Foil-assist manages the most expensive part of the speed curve — wave-making drag — while keeping landing-craft behavior: foils retract for harbors, ramps, and shallow water. And the physics precedent is 55 years old: USS Plainview, the U.S. Navy's 212-ft, 310-ton hydrofoil, flew at 40 knots in 1969. What was missing then was affordable control — which is software now, and it's the layer Navier owns.

**Q23. Why can a Navier cargo vessel serve islands big ships can't?**
Most islands lack deep berths and container cranes. The cargo line is designed for drive-on / drive-off loading at any hard ramp — no crane, no terminal. Every jetty becomes a port. That is precisely why islands pay the most for the slowest service today, and why the network that already earns on passengers gets to price that gap first.

**Q24. Why is the energy multiple bigger on the bigger boat — 13× vs 7×?**
Both figures are bottom-up on the operator's own schedule (16 nm legs, 8 daily runs, 300 days, in-market prices). Foiling cuts drag roughly 6× on either hull; efficient electric propulsion does the rest. The multiple then scales with how thirsty the diesel it replaces is: the Targa 32 burns ~2.4 L/nm, the Princess 55 ~10.9 L/nm — so the 20-seat electric N45 shows the bigger multiple. Net of all three Navier lines (energy, maintenance, software), the N45 runs $480K/yr cheaper than the Princess 55; the N30 $80K/yr cheaper than the Targa 32. Physics, not pricing. (Sources: instrumented dealer sea-trial for the Targa; manufacturer burn data for the Princess; battery spec ÷ range for the Naviers; electricity at $0.30/kWh — mid of STELCO Maldives' $0.21–$0.43 commercial band; STO diesel $1.50/L.)

**Q27 — How do you scale manufacturing without becoming a shipyard?**
Fabless — the NVIDIA model. Navier engineers the platform and supplies partner shipyards (JV structures in the Gulf, India, wherever demand sits) with mold designs and integration part kits — never the design intelligence, which stays in-house. Shipyards do what they're good at: build and customize superstructures (ferry, cargo, patrol). Shipyards are expensive, slow to stand up, and low-margin — we keep the high-margin physics layer and software layer. "We are not in the business of building chairs and tables."

**Q28 — Doesn't hydrofoiling limit payload?**
For passengers: no practical constraint — an N30 carries what any luxury boat of its size carries, and the space is generous. For cargo, two modes: urgent/light freight flies on full foil; heavy loads use foil-assist — carrying roughly what a displacement hull carries while keeping speed up (~40 kn) and saving ~15–30% on energy. That's why the large-cargo rungs of the ladder are designed foil-assist-first.

**Q29 — How big can a hydrofoil get? Could you build a 400-footer?**
Same question as "how big can an airplane get" — nothing in the physics stops you. The practical limits are materials/structures and takeoff power (peak power to lift the hull). The U.S. Navy built and flew a 220-ft, 310-ton hydrofoil in 1969 (USS Plainview) — what was missing then was control, which is exactly the layer Navier owns. At the large-cargo end, foil-assist is usually the right engineering answer anyway (see Q28).

**Q30 — What's the exit? How do we get liquidity?**
Philosophy first: build a profitable business that doesn't depend on the next venture round — sign national fleets and compound. Paths: IPO at scale is the primary route. Strategic interest spans defense primes, global shipping lines, recreational marine majors, and large tech companies returning to hardware. Structurally, the platform decomposes into independently valuable layers — recreational technology licensing, defense, the software/fleet-OS layer, and efficiency technology for shipping incumbents — so strategic value doesn't hinge on one acquirer wanting everything. And remember what acquirers actually buy: backlog and order books — which is exactly what our signed-demand pipeline builds. (Per standing rule: no valuations, no round terms in writing.)

**Q36 — Why is the ocean band so wide ($0.03–0.50/kg is a 15× spread)? Shouldn't you normalize by distance ($/kg-mile)?**
The spread is shipment size + lane type + market cycle — not distance. The $0.03 floor is a full 40-ft container on a dense trunk lane in a soft market (roughly $600–750 per box across 22–25 tonnes of payload). The $0.50 ceiling is small-parcel freight — less-than-container-load, short-sea, island feeder lanes — where handling dominates the bill. On top of that, spot container rates have swung roughly 10× within three years (2021 peak vs 2023 trough).

Distance-normalized, the gap survives. Same lane, Shanghai→LA (~10,500 km): air at $3.50/kg ≈ $0.33 per tonne-km; full-container ocean at $0.075/kg ≈ $0.007 per tonne-km — about 45×, inside the 40–80× on the slide. We quote per kilogram because that is how freight is actually bought; neither mode prices linearly with distance — terminal and handling costs are large fixed chunks.

The point that matters for Navier: on our corridors — short island and coastal lanes — ocean sits at the top of its band ($0.20–0.50/kg parcel/feeder reality) and air, where it exists at all, at the top of its band too. Our illustrative $0.75–1.50/kg is priced against that local reality, not against a Shanghai→LA container.

**Q37 — Why can't you compete with container ships? What about transatlantic lanes?**
We don't try. A container ship moving 10,000+ boxes across an ocean is unbeatable on cost per kilogram — and large freighter aircraft own the urgent end of trunk lanes. Both modes need scale and infrastructure that only exist on trunk routes. Navier cargo is a short-sea play: islands, coasts, and regional corridors, typically 30–100 nm, where neither megaship nor freighter jet operates — the freight moves on slow feeder boats or expensive small planes, at the worst prices in either band. That is the gap we price inside ($0.75–1.50/kg), and it is exactly where our passenger networks already put piers, chargers, and software. Never claim trunk-lane or transoceanic economics.
