## 2. The Problem

Water carries roughly 90% of global trade, yet maritime technology has barely moved in fifty years. Three costs keep the $1T industry stuck:

- **Expensive to move.** A displacement hull wastes ~90% of its energy fighting drag. Going fast on water costs about 15× more than going fast in a car — operators must choose speed or cost.
- **Expensive to build.** Every vessel is engineered from scratch. No standardization, no economies of scale.
- **Expensive to run.** A full crew on every boat, hands-on maintenance, low utilization. About 70% of a vessel’s lifetime cost is operations.

## 3. The Platform

Navier’s answer is one lever per cost:

- **Hydrofoiling +****efficient propulsion****.** Foils lift the hull above the water, erasing ~90% of drag. Fully electric, a $59 diesel leg becomes $8 of electricity; across a year on our operator’s own schedule and fares, that is 7× lower energy cost per mile — and 13× on the N45, whose diesel counterpart is thirstier still. Run hybrid, the same foil efficiency still cuts fuel burn deeply on routes where charging does not yet exist.
- **One standard platform.** NavierOS on a common hull-and-powertrain core, configured per mission and built in volume.
- **Autonomy + software upkeep.** The software already flies the boat and monitors its own health; every release shrinks the crew’s role and the maintenance bill.

One architecture carries this across two breakthrough platforms: Pioneer, in production and earning revenue with paying customers today, and Quanta, now in pre-production.

**N30 Pioneer — unlocking island transportation.** The all-electric N30 Pioneer is Navier’s first production platform, with a 70nm range. Its efficiency, stability, low wake, low noise, and software-defined operation make it uniquely suited to:

- Island and coastal passenger transportation
- Resort amenity boats
- Airport, hotel, and resort connections
- Inter-island routes
- Premium commuter networks
- High-value short-distance cargo

The Pioneer fleet is what generated Navier’s 10,000 operational hours and $10M of revenue. Our signed Maldives agreement begins with an initial deployment phase and provides a pathway toward a network of 100 vessels connecting airports, resorts, villas, and local islands. Since the announcement, inbound interest has followed from French Polynesia, Cambodia, Thailand, Mauritius, and other island and coastal markets. The opportunity is no longer to sell vessels individually — it is to repeat a full network playbook across multiple geographies.

**Quanta — changing what is possible at sea.** Quanta is Navier’s hybrid-electric breakthrough — a step-change in maritime endurance, not an incremental upgrade. Typical vessels in this category reach their limit at 300–400 nautical miles; by extending our proven electric architecture with hybrid-electric propulsion, Quanta achieves a mission range of ~2,000 nautical miles at 20 knots — 5× the range of anything in its class — with a 35-knot dash, foilborne in seas up to Sea State 4. Fast vessels have always traded speed, range, and efficiency against one another; Quanta is the first in its class that does not have to choose. And because it flies above the waves rather than slamming through them, it holds two qualities no planing hull can: a stable ride that keeps sensors and payloads working at speed, and an electric-quiet approach. That redraws the operating envelope and expands Navier from regional transportation into:

- Modular defense and security missions
- Distributed naval logistics
- Long-distance island and coastal transportation
- High-throughput short-sea cargo
- Persistent autonomous operations
- Maritime surveillance and communications

The platform combines hydrofoils, active flight controls, hybrid diesel and electric propulsion, autonomy, communications, and modular payload capacity — preserving the advantages of an electric architecture while adding the endurance, redundancy, and onboard power that long-range operations demand.

Quanta does not simply introduce another vessel — it changes what can be accomplished through the sea, and it is not a defense boat but a new class of vessel that opens the defense market. A Quanta can self-deploy over long distances, remain on mission for days, fulfill rapid deliveries for contested logistics, cover far larger areas with fewer assets, and reduce dependence on vulnerable refueling and logistics chains. This will fundamentally change naval warfare and deterrence. And where today's autonomous vessels are single-mission designs, Quanta is one platform carrying defense, mobility, and logistics — partners integrate their own systems on top. For commercial markets, that same endurance opens long-distance island transportation, remote resort access, and ferry and cargo routes that electric vessels alone cannot serve — and it is those commercial fleets that fund the platform defense buyers need. Quanta has been invited to military exercises and is slated for an unmanned Atlantic crossing at year end. The plan to build Quanta was announced in late 2025; the vessel itself — built, on the water, and in sea trials — has not yet been shown publicly.

Four larger platforms (45 ft, 80 ft, 120 ft, 180 ft) are in design on the same core, enabling more passengers or cargo at a lower cost per passenger mile.

## 6. Business Model

Navier’s long-term business is the complete maritime network:

• Physical infrastructure — high-efficiency vessels, charging and energy systems, service, maintenance, deployment, ports, and shipyards

• Digital infrastructure — fleet intelligence, diagnostics, autonomy, dispatch, route optimization, and network operations

• Commercial infrastructure — asset partners, local operators, customer aggregation, and repeatable deployment models

Together these create multiple revenue layers — vessel sales, software, maintenance, technical services, network operations, data, and recurring platform fees. Three lines dominate today, all on one installed base:

- **Hardware.** Platform and vessel sales — direct, or via shipyard license at 44–50% of vessel value. Hardware sells once.
- **Software.** NavierOS — fleet management, experiences and multichannel booking, the captain app, remote diagnostics and analytics, continuous over-the-air updates — an annual per-vessel SaaS fee: $60K–$120K per hull for fleets, with a lighter single-vessel tier from ~$10K.
- **Network share.** A 5–15% fee on journey GMV, paid by the asset holder or operator in each market.

Revenue = units delivered × ASP, plus installed base × recurring (SaaS + network share). 

In the Maldives, Navier earns ~$1.0M per vessel sale plus ~$262K per vessel per year recurring. The network is the moat: every new route adds infrastructure, operating data, customers, and fleet density — the same compounding Tesla built with vehicles-plus-charging and SpaceX with launch-plus-Starlink.

**Unit economics, against the incumbent.** Same price, same fares — only the running costs change. On our Maldives operator’s own schedule and fares (8 daily legs, 300 days a year), the N30 runs at 7× lower energy cost per mile than its 30-ft diesel counterpart, and the electric N45 13× lower than the Princess 55 it replaces. All-in, a Navier vessel is roughly 2–3× cheaper to run — even carrying a software line the diesels don’t have: $60K–$120K per hull per year, paid back to Navier as recurring revenue.

The field, for contrast: the commercial electric-foiler field tops out at roughly 70 nautical miles of range, and none of it is built in America; no other builder has 10,000 commercial hours on the water or a long-range hull in trials. Software and autonomy are easy to claim — country of build, hours on the water, and range are checkable, and each is Navier’s alone.

## 8. Strategy & the Long Term

The United States ranked 19th in global commercial shipbuilding output in 2025. Navier is a chance to build a great American — and global — maritime company: serving American maritime needs and exporting American technology, not a handful of naval vessels at a time but complete commercial and defense fleets supplied around the world.

Deployments in the Maldives, Cambodia, and the UAE point to the same conclusion: cutting the energy cost of moving people and goods on water is a universal need. So we build the vessel and the network together — vessels, software, routes, charging, infrastructure, operations. The vessel opens a market; the network keeps it.

Quanta extends the same core technology into long-range cargo, logistics, defense, and persistent maritime operations — commercial and strategic markets served on one platform.

The second act is cargo. Between air freight ($8 trillion of goods a year at $2.50–4.50 per kilogram — dense trunk lanes at the floor, thin and urgent lanes at the ceiling) and container shipping ($7 trillion at $0.03–0.50 per kilogram — full containers on trunk lanes at the floor, small parcels on island lanes at the ceiling) sits a 40–80× price gap that no vessel has ever served — every island and short-sea corridor lives in it, and small island states pay twice the world average to move their imports through it (UNCTAD). The play is dedicated foiling freighters for short-sea and island corridors, built on the same proven core — designed in two modes: urgent freight flies full-foil, while heavy loads ride foil-assist, the foils carrying part of the weight to cut the power bill; and they load drive-on, drive-off at any hard ramp — no cranes, no container terminal. Example economics price the lane midway between the ocean and air bands — $0.75–1.50 per kilogram, a fraction of air rates and a healthy multiple of ocean — for delivery in hours, not weeks. The way in costs nothing new: in the pilot phase we trial night freight on the passenger fleet — same piers, same charging, same software — proving the cargo model corridor by corridor, where the economics clear, before the first freighter launches. Prior attempts at fast waterborne freight failed because they were cargo-first, with no revenue until the largest ship in the plan was afloat; we sequence the other way — passenger networks first, night-freight trials next, dedicated freighters (N80–N180) as the network scales.

The map grows from here: regional joint ventures in development in the UAE, fleet discussions underway with several island nations, and an expected presence in the Indian Ocean port ecosystem forming around the Maldives. Building these fleets positions Navier as a global American maritime company, with a commercial presence across some of the world’s most important maritime regions.

## 13. Risks and How We Manage Them

- **Production ramp.** The near-term fleet is built on a certified, already-in-production platform; deliveries are contracted and sequenced (first vessel October 2026, 20 by end of 2027), and the shipyard-license model adds capacity without adding factories.
- **Capital intensity.** Navier does not own the fleets. Asset partners finance the vessels (JIH in the Maldives); Navier earns on the sale and on the recurring software and network lines.
- **Regulatory and certification.** Maritime certification is measured in months, not the decade aviation requires — the first fleet is already certified and delivered across multiple jurisdictions.
- **Round timing.** The two-stage structure de-risks the raise itself: the first close keeps execution at full speed while the full round — already in advanced discussions with a lead — completes.
